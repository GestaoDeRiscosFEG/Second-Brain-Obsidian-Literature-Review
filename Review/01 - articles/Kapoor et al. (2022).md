### Kapoor et al. (2022)

#### Reference (ABNT)
KAPOOR, Sayash, et al. Weaving privacy and power: on the privacy practices of labor organizers in the U.S. technology industry. **Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction**, v. 6, n. CSCW2, art. 472, p. 1-33, 2022. DOI: 10.1145/3555573.

#### Objective
To investigate the privacy and security practices of labor organizers in the U.S. computer technology industry, examining how these practices adapted to widespread remote work, corporate surveillance software, and distributed digital communications.

#### Research questions or hypotheses
How do labor organizers in the technology industry evaluate and respond to privacy risks? What are the specific collective and individual security goals of tech organizers? What threats and trade-offs do organizers encounter between technical operational security and collective community accessibility? What impact has the shift to remote work had on their organizational practices?

#### Method
*   **Type:** Semi-structured qualitative interview study
*   **Application:** In-depth interviews with 29 labor organizers representing 17 non-unionized technology companies in the United States, including corporate tech employees, subcontracted workers, and app-based platform contractors
*   **Type of work:** Remote and distributed knowledge work

#### Main findings
The transition to remote work accelerated the adoption of commercial collaboration tools (e.g., Slack, Google Workspace, Zoom), which simultaneously expanded workplace surveillance capabilities (bossware) by management. Organizers face severe structural threats, including employer retaliation, termination, career blacklisting, and doxxing. However, technical privacy practices are heavily constrained by social trade-offs: while end-to-end encrypted tools (Signal, CryptPad, Keybase) provide superior security against employer surveillance, they create steep onboarding friction for coworkers. Organizers frequently compromise technical security in favor of accessible, familiar platforms to foster participation. Furthermore, emotional labor and burnout emerge as primary hazards, driven by constant lateral conflict, fear of infiltration, and the complete blur of personal and organizing boundaries in remote domestic spaces.

#### Identified risks
*   **Classification:** Psychosocial, Organizational
*   **Specific risk factors:** Pervasive employer surveillance, keystroke logging, retaliatory termination, career sabotage, fear of corporate espionage, lateral coworker conflict, doxxing, harassment, high mental workload, emotional exhaustion, and burnout
*   **Impacts and consequences:** Chronic mental and emotional exhaustion, severe anxiety, termination of employment, blacklisting, isolation within toxic workplace environments, and impaired personal relationships

#### Risk management practices
Deployment of worker-managed digital defense protocols (segregating corporate hardware from personal devices, utilizing pseudonymous accounts, and setting ephemeral message retention). Implementation of community moderation norms to reduce toxic lateral conflict and de-escalate emotional tension. Strategic adoption of safety in numbers by coordinating collective actions to shield precarious and visa-dependent workers from individual retaliation. Establishment of emotional debriefing and mutual care networks among organizers to prevent burnout.

#### Relation to regulations
The study critically assesses the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) of 1935, highlighting its obsolescence in protecting digital communications and concerted worker activity on employer-monitored channels. It also notes the complete exclusion of gig economy contractors from statutory collective bargaining protections.

#### Gaps pointed out by the authors
Extant human-computer interaction (HCI) and security literature largely frames privacy as an individualized, consumer-centric choice rather than a collective, adversarial labor process. There is a lack of research examining how algorithmic management and remote workplace surveillance impact worker solidarity and organizing efficacy over time. Further investigation is needed to design collaborative digital tools that embed collective privacy features without requiring specialized cryptographic expertise.

#### Other relevant information
The authors conceptualize privacy as socially situated, demonstrating that organizers deliberately accept higher technical surveillance risks to preserve social inclusion, trust, and movement growth.
