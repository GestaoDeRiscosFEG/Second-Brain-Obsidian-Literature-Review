### McAllister et al. (2022)

#### Reference (ABNT)
MCALLISTER, Megan J., et al. The effect of training and workstation adjustability on teleworker discomfort during the COVID-19 pandemic. **Applied Ergonomics**, v. 102, art. 103749, p. 1-8, 2022. DOI: 10.1016/j.apergo.2022.103749.

#### Objective
To evaluate the transition to telework among university faculty and staff during the COVID-19 pandemic, investigating the effects of individual home workstation setup, adjustability, and ergonomics training on work-related musculoskeletal discomfort in the domestic environment.

#### Research questions or hypotheses
Does transitioning to home telework increase daily computer screen time and reduce postural variation throughout the workday? Are home workstations equipped with adjustable ergonomic furniture associated with lower levels of reported discomfort? Does previous on-campus ergonomic training and workstation assessment translate into improved home office setups and reduced physical aches?

#### Method
*   **Type:** Cross-sectional quantitative survey
*   **Application:** Web-based survey administered to 131 faculty, staff, and administrative personnel at Queen's University (Canada) working remotely and utilizing computers for at least 50% of their daily shift during COVID-19 restrictions
*   **Type of work:** Work from home (telework)

#### Main findings
The emergency transition to telework degraded physical working conditions: 64.9% of respondents increased their daily time spent at the computer, 53.4% changed physical postures less frequently, and 51.9% experienced no change or a decrease in task variety. Widespread physical deterioration was documented: 51% of workers reported worsening discomfort in one or more anatomical regions (35% neck, 34% arm, 32% back), and 24% reported brand-new discomfort that did not exist prior to telework (18% back, 18% arm, 3% neck). Only 7% experienced an improvement. While workstation quality scores were positively associated with ergonomic training (p = 0.08), loglinear analyses revealed that neither training nor adjustable furniture alone eliminated discomfort. The authors conclude that effective risk mitigation requires a tripartite approach combining adjustable ergonomic furniture, practical training on equipment adjustment, and individualized virtual workstation assessments.

#### Identified risks
*   **Classification:** Ergonomic, Organizational
*   **Specific risk factors:** Unsuitable domestic workstations (working at dining tables, sofas, and armchairs), non-adjustable chairs, coupled laptop keyboard and monitor configurations, sustained static neck flexion, prolonged sitting, and reduced frequency of postural adjustments
*   **Impacts and consequences:** Work-related musculoskeletal disorders, persistent cervicalgia, thoracic pain, chronic lumbar backache, arm and wrist strain, and physical fatigue

#### Risk management strategies
Development and deployment of a standardized enterprise telework package providing all remote employees with a baseline set of adjustable ergonomic hardware (adjustable office chair, external monitor, external keyboard, and mouse). Mandatory ergonomics training educating employees on how to properly adjust their equipment and maintain neutral spinal alignment. Implementation of virtual ergonomic assessments combining self-reports, worker photos/videos, and live consultations with trained ergonomists. Encouraging micro-breaks and postural position changes every 30 minutes.

#### Tools and techniques
- [[Discomfort Body Map]]: Visual diagnostic tool highlighting 23 body regions where participants indicate presence and quantify severity of discomfort on a continuum from "No discomfort at all" to "Worst discomfort imaginable" before and during telework.
- [[Workstation Score Assessment]]: Composite scoring index evaluating presence of core ergonomic furniture elements (height-adjustable desk, adjustable chair, standalone monitor, external mouse, and keyboard).

not reported

#### Gaps pointed out by the authors
The study relied on retrospective subjective self-reports to evaluate pre-pandemic baseline discomfort, introducing potential recall bias. Ergonometrics training was categorized broadly without controlling for duration, pedagogical depth, or delivery format (online asynchronous vs. live in-person). The sample size was relatively modest (n = 131) and limited to a single academic institution, requiring caution when generalizing findings to other knowledge sectors. Future research should evaluate standardized, company-subsidized home office equipment interventions longitudinally.

#### Other relevant information
The authors emphasize that merely providing adjustable equipment without educating workers on how to adjust it to their own anthropometry fails to mitigate musculoskeletal symptoms.
