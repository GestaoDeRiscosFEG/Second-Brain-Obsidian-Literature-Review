### Sein et al. (2026)

#### Reference (ABNT)
GOÑI SEIN, José Luis, et al. An international perspective on occupational health and safety challenges for remote workers. In: **Telework and the Law: International Perspectives on Labour Law**, p. 200-216, 2026.

#### Objective
To examine the international legal, regulatory, and preventive challenges confronting occupational safety and health (OSH) for cross-border and domestic remote workers, analyzing the crisis of traditional private international law conflict rules, the reconceptualization of working time, the rise of algorithmic surveillance, and the practical implementation of preventive obligations.

#### Research questions or hypotheses
How can occupational risk prevention frameworks designed for physical, geographically fixed workplaces adapt to cross-border and distributed remote work? Does the anytime-anywhere character of telework require a fundamental reconceptualization of statutory working and rest times? What are the OSH implications of automated algorithmic management and electronic surveillance on remote employee mental health? How can employers conduct lawful workstation risk assessments without infringing on the constitutional inviolability of the home and personal privacy?

#### Method
*   **Type:** Qualitative legal and comparative doctrine review
*   **Application:** Legal analysis of European Union employment directives, Rome I Regulation, European Framework Agreement on Telework, Spanish Distance Working Act 10/2021, and comparative European jurisprudence regarding cross-border remote work and digital risk management
*   **Type of work:** Remote work, cross-border telework, and mobile digital work

#### Main findings
The detachment of work from a physical, fixed premises destabilizes the core foundations of labor law and private international law conflict rules (such as Article 8 of the Rome I Regulation), creating severe uncertainty regarding which country's OSH standards govern cross-border teleworkers. Remote work redefines temporal boundaries, generating constant availability pressure and invading private domestic time, particularly in knowledge work evaluated by performance objectives rather than logged hours. Digital performance monitoring (keystroke logging, webcam tracking, and algorithmic surveillance) significantly exacerbates psychosocial hazards, inducing tecno-stress, anxiety, and profound mistrust. Furthermore, fulfilling the employer's statutory duty to assess home working environments conflicts directly with constitutional rights to privacy and the inviolability of the home. While statutory self-assessments provide a practical bridge, they risk transferring preventive responsibilities to workers and failing to assess non-desk domestic spaces (kitchens, hallways) where work-related accidents frequently occur.

#### Identified risks
*   **Classification:** Psychosocial, Organizational, Ergonomic
*   **Specific risk factors:** Unpredictable on-call availability, excessive overtime, blurred work-life boundaries, invasive algorithmic surveillance, biometric monitoring, digital moral and sexual harassment (cyberbullying, doxxing, defamatory messaging), loss of worker autonomy, awkward posture from display screens, and absence of professional OSH inspections
*   **Impacts and consequences:** Chronic occupational stress, severe anxiety, burnout, psychological distress, musculoskeletal disorders, eye strain, tension headaches, and enterprise civil liability resulting from unmitigated remote work accidents

#### Risk management strategies
Formulating harmonized international and European OSH directives that establish non-derogable minimum safety baselines for remote workers regardless of physical location. Statutory recognition and collective bargaining enforcement of the right to digital disconnection. Establishing secure digital daily time recording systems accessible to labor inspectorates. Involving trade union representatives in the governance and auditing of workplace algorithms. Designing structured online theoretical and practical OSH training modules adapted to telework. Implementing standardized home office self-assessment questionnaires supported by expert technical feedback. Establishing strict zero-tolerance corporate protocols against cyber-harassment with clear reporting and conciliation procedures.

#### Relation to regulations
The study carries out extensive analysis of European Union Council Directive 89/391/EEC (Framework Directive), Directive 2003/88/EC (Working Time Directive), Regulation (EC) No 593/2008 (Rome I Regulation), and the 2002 European Framework Agreement on Telework. It evaluates national transposition statutes, primarily the Spanish Distance Working Act (Law 10/2021), Law 31/1995 on the Prevention of Occupational Risks, and Article 88 of Organic Law 3/2018 on Digital Disconnection, reviewing critical judicial rulings on home inspection limits (SAN 44/2022).

#### Gaps pointed out by the authors
Existing conflict-of-law rules and OSH statutes continue to rely on the fiction of a regular, physical workplace, creating an acute regulatory gap for transnational remote employees. There is a lack of European legislative harmonization defining employer preventive obligations and digital availability limits. Current self-assessment protocols in telework are vulnerable to superficial execution, failing to capture complex digital psychosocial hazards and algorithmic management pressures. Judicial doctrine has historically resisted fully virtual preventive training, creating uncertainty for mobile remote workers. Further research must explore legal mechanisms to establish direct communication channels between labor inspectorates and decentralized digital workforces.

#### Other relevant information
The authors emphasize that algorithmic management should itself be legally categorized as a primary psychosocial risk-generating factor that must be explicitly evaluated in corporate risk prevention plans.
