### Chen (2024)

#### Reference (ABNT)
CHEN, Guang X. Working hours, shift, and remote work by industry and occupation in U.S. full-time workers. **Workplace Health & Safety**, v. 72, n. 9, p. 392-400, 2024. DOI: 10.1177/21650799241240212.

#### Objective
To evaluate working hours and non-daytime shift patterns across occupations and industry sectors among full-time workers in the United States, determine the association between work hours and sleep duration, and assess the prevalence and sectoral distribution of remote work adopted in response to the COVID-19 pandemic.

#### Research questions or hypotheses
What are the baseline patterns of working hours and shift schedules across diverse occupations and industries in the United States? What is the empirical relationship between daily working hours and sleep duration? What was the adoption rate of remote work across occupational categories during the pandemic?

#### Method
*   **Type:** Cross-sectional quantitative survey analysis
*   **Application:** Nationally representative survey microdata from the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) administered by the U.S. Census Bureau for the Bureau of Labor Statistics, examining historical trends (2011 to 2019) and COVID-19 pandemic adaptations (2020)
*   **Type of work:** Remote, on-site, and shift-based work

#### Main findings
Between 2011 and 2019, full-time U.S. workers averaged 8.1 hours of work per workday. Emergency medical technicians and paramedics worked the longest daily hours (10.4 hours), while truck transportation was the industry with the highest daily average (9.2 hours). Approximately 13.8% of full-time workers regularly worked non-daytime shifts, with the highest prevalence in protective service occupations (41.7%) and food/beverage services (28.6%). Sleep duration averaged 7.9 hours on workdays and 9.3 hours on non-workdays, with linear regression confirming that sleep duration is significantly and negatively associated with working hours and worker age. In 2020, 34.0% of full-time workers engaged in remote work due to the pandemic. Remote work adoption exhibited extreme sectoral polarization, reaching 72.2% in business and financial operations but only 1.6% in protective service and construction/extraction occupations.

#### Identified risks
*   **Classification:** Organizational, Physical, Psychosocial
*   **Specific risk factors:** Extended working hours, non-daytime shifts, night shifts, chronic sleep deficiency, occupational fatigue, acute exhaustion, and sudden transition to remote work
*   **Impacts and consequences:** Chronic sleep restriction, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, obesity, metabolic dysfunction, workplace injuries, reduced mental health, and elevated occupational mortality

#### Risk management strategies
Implementation of comprehensive fatigue risk management programs within organizations. Optimization of shift scheduling and limit setting on consecutive long workdays. Work redesign to promote adequate sleep hygiene and rest recovery. Formulation of evidence-based best practice guidelines for remote work to mitigate blurred work-life boundaries and prevent extended remote work hours.

#### Relation to regulations
The study discusses the absence of federal statutory limits on daily work hours for most adult civilian occupations under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), contrasting this with industry-specific federal hours-of-service regulations (e.g., Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration rules). It highlights the need for public health and labor policies addressing work-hour limits and fatigue.

#### Gaps pointed out by the authors
Previous research on long working hours and health impacts primarily relied on small, occupation-specific cohorts, lacking nationally representative baseline data across all industries and occupations. As remote work becomes an enduring arrangement, further empirical research is needed to investigate which operational facets of remote work are protective versus hazardous to health and safety, supporting the development of organizational best practice standards. In addition, the ATUS one-day 24-hour diary methodology (4:00 a.m. to 4:00 a.m.) may truncate the recorded hours of workers with non-standard nocturnal schedules.
